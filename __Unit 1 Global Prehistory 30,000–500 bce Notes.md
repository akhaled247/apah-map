1. **Unit introduction; 11 works, 12 images**  
   1. Our timeline for completing this unit: 3 Periods  
   2. Time period 30,000-500 bce  
      1. Paleolithic   
         1. 2.5 million-10,000 bce  
         2. 3 divisions   
            1. Lower Paleolithic (old) ca  2.7 million-300,000 years ago  
            2. Middle Paleolithic ca 300,000 to 45,000 years ago  
            3. Upper Paleolithic (new) ca 45,000-10,000 years ago  
         3. Upper Paleolithic  
            1. Modern behaviors  
               1. Cave art  
               2. Tools of stone, antlers, bone, ivory  
               3. Hunting  
      2. Mesolithic   
         1. approximately from 10,000 BCE to 8,000 BCE  
         2. transitional phase between the Paleolithic Age and the Neolithic Age, time varied by region  
         3. hunting, fishing, and food gathering; later there was some domestication of animals  
      3. Neolithic  
         1. roughly 8,000 B.C.E. to 3,000 B.C.E.   
         2. Continuation of mesolithic behaviors  
         3. Development of agriculture  
         4. Beginning of settlements  
   3. [Geographic Regions](https://earth.google.com/web/@11.8000413,0,285.36247872a,12160162.36537201d,30y,0h,0t,0r/data=MicKJQojCiExQjlwSWlzSjN3YjBzSFBGT0JwcDk0TG9pcUhITHhaRDI)  
   4. Cultural overviews  
   5. **AP CED Topics/Essential Knowledge**  
      1. **Cultural Influences on Prehistoric Art**  
         1. Early art is found *worldwide*  
         2. Art shares features such as man’s place in the natural world  
         3. Periods before the written record are often defined in terms of geological eras  
            1. Paleolithic  
            2. Mesolithic  
            3. Neolithic  
         4. Behaviors and expressions influenced by changing environments  
         5. Globally, the earliest peoples were small groups of hunter-gatherers  
         6. Survival was main concern  
         7. Practical tools were accompanied by objects of unknown purpose  
         8. Ritual and spiritual items  
         9. Art related to:  
            1. Food production/hunting/gathering  
            2. Animal husbandry  
            3. Settlement  
            4. Status  
            5. Burial  
         10. Art from varied regions demonstrated  
             1. Awareness of astronomical cycles, such as equinoxes and solstices  
             2. Exploitation of available resources  
      2. **Materials, Processes, and Techniques in Prehistoric Art**  
         1. Early art and media spread from Africa and Asia to other parts of the globe with the spread of humanity  
         2. Art created roughly 77,000 years ago were rock paintings and carved sculptures  
         3. Geometric patterns and living things \- 2D  
         4. 3D art tended to be large objects, monuments and environments  
         5. In prehistoric China, ritual objects were created in jade  
         6. Saudi Arabia \- funerary steles  
         7. Ceramics, particularly Japan and China, Iran  
         8. Asian land bridges in the Pacific allowed migration of ceramics   
         9. Europe \- cave paintings and small carved figurines as well as monumental stone assemblages  
            1. Ritualistic works  
            2. Trying to influence the natural world  
         10. Americas \- migration from Asia making first made sculptures from animal bone and later from clay, with animals and sacred humans as dominant subject matter  
      3. **Theories and Interpretations of Prehistoric Art**  
         1. Art historians have developed greater understanding of prehistory through collaboration with social and physical scientists  
         2. Carbon 14 dating, and archaeological excavations helped to discover connections between art across the globe.   
         3. Object  comparisons, ethnographic analogies and religious history help to establish theories of function and meaning.   
         4. Shamanism has been a one of the earliest and persistent spiritualistic approaches to religion  
         5. Modern stratigraphic archaeology (recording precisely each level and location of all objects) begun in the early 1900’s, supports understandings of peoples, cultures and their art has traveled the globe.  
         6. Technology, survival strategies, food sources, man’s shaping of the environment all help to define artistic expression.   
            1. Comparing works, imagery, materials help to show patterns  
            2. Ethnography and cross cultural comparisons help to establish and test theories.

1. **Apollo 11 Stones**, Namibia. c. 25,500–25,300 bce. Charcoal on stone.  
   1. Form (visual analysis)  
      1. Stone painted with charcoal, ocher and white  
      2. Simplified silhouette of an animal form (unknown) drawn on surface  
      3. Rendered in profile view to clearly represent the animal.  
      4. Small in size 4.3 x 5”  
   2. Function  
      1. Possibly *sympathetic magic*, allowing control over animals  
      2. Not known for certain  
   3. Content  
      1. Representation of animal form  
      2. Stylized; not natural  
      3. Mystical content?  
   4. Context (contextual analysis)  
      1. Found in a cave or rock shelter in the Huns mountains in Namibia (in sw Africa) in 1969  
         1. Evidence appears to show that this was the location of an ongoing settlement that had places of ritual significance  
      2. Named after the successful Apollo 11 mission, the first to take man to the moon.   
      3. The stones were discovered by German archeologist W.E. Wendt  
      4. Dated to Upper Paleolithic time period \- these are some of the earliest known rock paintings in Africa  
      5. Nomadic peoples \- small size allows portability for the mobile hunter gatherers.  
      6. Stone Age artists from paleo through neolithic periods rendered mostly animals, rarely people  
           
2. **Great Hall of the Bulls** Lascaux, France. Paleolithic Europe. 15,000–13,000 bce. Rock painting.   
   1. Form (visual analysis)  
      1. Large in scale (largest bull is 11’6” long)  
      2. Some silhouetted with color, others outlined  
      3. Images are usually drawn in a manner to best represent the animal, profile with all four legs seen and if applicable, horns in twisted perspective.  
      4. Use of twisted perspective \- animal from side, but horns from a frontal view. You may also find this referred to as a composite view.  
      5. Drawn, painted, blown materials create the artworks  
      6. Overlapping shapes could be an indication of various artists working over a period of time or possibly showing an awareness of naturalism depicting some animals in front of others.   
      7. Varying sizes  
   2. Function  
      1. No certain meaning, variety of theories, but most have been discredited over time  
      2. Ritualistic,hunting magic?   
      3. The location of the artwork within the caves indicates a possible ritualistic function as many are far from the cave’s entrance.  
      4. It has been suggested that there was a connection between the  mortal and spiritual worlds that was made through the act of painting.   
   3. Content  
      1. Representational, yet non-natural images of animals  
      2. Variety of recognizable animals give indication of wildlife present  
      3. Overlapping of forms can give a feeling of naturalism and depth  
   4. Context (contextual analysis)  
      1. Located at a cave in Lascaux France  
      2. Discovered in 1940  
      3. Caves closed to public, but nearby historical center has replications  
      4. Similar to some 350 other caves, mostly in southern France and northern Spain  
      5. Most paintings hundreds of feet from cave openings  
      6. Holes in walls might have held scaffolding  
      7. Animal fat lamps found  
      8. Theory \- Breuil, used ethnography \- hunting magic  
           
3. **Camelid sacrum in the shape of a canine.** Tequixquiac, central Mexico. 14,000–7000 bce. Bone.  
   Pronunciation: sounds like techies **key** ac  
   1. Form (visual analysis)  
      1. Hand carved bone from camelid (animal related to camels and llamas)  
      2. The bone was a natural choice as it already bears a resemblance to the subject  
      3. It is nearly symmetrical  
      4. Natural coloration  
   2. Function  
      1. Possibly had some sacred or religious purpose as the sacrum held importance to later meso American cultures.  This may have been the reason that this particular bone was chosen. It held spiritual significance.  
      2. Created before the development of written language, it is impossible to know for certain the meaning to the artist.  
   3. Content  
      1. The head of a dog carved from the sacrum of a camelid. The sacrum is a bone at the base of the spine  
   4. Context (contextual analysis)  
      1. Currently housed in Mexico City's National Museum of Anthropology  
      2. Most likely carved by a nomadic hunter of large game during the upper paleolithic period, it is one of the oldest examples of art from the Americas  
      3. Discovered in 1870 in Tequixquiac, just north of Mexico City by an engineer working on a drainage project  
      4. Difficult to date as stratigraphic analysis wasn’t done at the site during the time of discovery.

      2. Patterning with dots prevalent

   4. Context (contextual analysis)

      2. Area has high concentration of cave paintings

      4. Brenans was not the first to discover, had been visited by   
         Europeans previously.   
      5. Indiginous peoples already knew the sites and served as guides.   
      6. Archaeologist Henri Lhote made many visits to the site and wrote a book that is still today an important text on archaeology.

7. **Jade cong** Liangzhu, China. 3300–2200 bce. Carved jade. (pronounce like batsong minus the “ba”) (the city: lee-ang-jew)  
   1. Form (visual analysis)  
      1. Made from jade  
         1. Metamorphic rock  
         2. Harder than steel  
         3. Shades of green, white, yellow, red  
         4. Its strength allows it to be carved into intricate designs  
         5. Must be abraded with sand because of hardness  
      2. Shape bears a resemblance to a hollow tube embedded into a rectangular block  
      3. Faces carved into corners  
      4. Lines \- repeated with a degree of regularity  
   2. Function  
      1. Some type of ritual object  
      2. Found in graves around the perimeter of a the deceased  
      3. Exact function unknown, possibly to serve a connection between earthly and spiritual realms.    
   3. Content  
      1. Mask-like faces, possible spirits or deities?  
      2. Possible reference to earth and heavens  
         1. Square \- earth  
         2. Circle \- heavens  
   4. Context (contextual analysis)  
      1. Congs were ancient Chinese artifacts found in graves  
      2. Neolithic time period, culture was becoming more settled and agricultural  
      3. The Liangzhu culture was well known for its jadework.   
      4. Depending on size and complexity of the design, and due to the difficulty in working with jade, the process could take months and be very expensive  
      5. Often accompanied by discs called Bi (sounds like B)  
           
8. **Stonehenge** Wiltshire, UK. Neolithic Europe. c. 2500–1600 bce. Sandstone. 2 images  
   Great video/explanation/description:   
   [https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fx-KrvuiafE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fx-KrvuiafE)  
   1. Form (visual analysis)  
      1. Large ditch (henge) approximately 360 feet in diameter  
      2. 56 pits slightly more than 3’ in diameter  
      3. There were three stages of development. Upright wooden posts were added in the center and at the two entrances during stage 2  
      4. Stones  
         1. Blue stones (volcanic and igneous rocks) placed in Aubrey holes  
         2. Sarsen stones (silicified sandstone) placed in 108’ diameter circle  
      5. Post and lintel construction  
      6. Tongue and groove  
      7. Mortise and tenon joints  
   2. Function  
      1. After the second stage of construction, it was used, at least in part for burials. Cremated remains are still buried in the Aubrey holes  
      2. Would appear to have a ritual significance as the axis of the structure is aligned with the sunrise of the summer solstice and the setting of the midwinter sun   
      3. Recent research suggests that is was possibly a place for healing of the sick and a pilgrimage site  
   3. Content  
      1. Non-representational, monumental architectural arrangement and sacred earthwork  
      2. Outer circle of thirty sarsen upright megaliths linked by continuous curved horizontal lintels  
      3. Inner horseshoe configuration of five grand sarsen trilithons (paired uprights with a lintel), with the central Giant Trilithon at the apex  
      4. Concentric settings of smaller bluestones arranged between and within the sarsen formations  
      5. Central axis aligned with the celestial calendar (summer solstice sunrise and winter solstice sunset)  
      6. Enclosing circular earthwork ditch and bank, Aubrey Holes, and ceremonial Avenue leading toward the River Avon  
   4. Context (contextual analysis)  
      1. One of the most recognizable monuments from the neolithic period  
      2. Located on the Salisbury plain in England  
      3. Built in at least three phases of construction  
         1. Stage 1 ca 3100 bce \- henge (the ditch)  
            1. The oldest part of the Stonehenge monument was built during the period from 3000 to 2935 BCE. It consists of a circular enclosure that is more than 330 feet (100 metres) in diameter, enclosing 56 pits called the Aubrey Holes, named after John Aubrey, who identified them in 1666\. The ditch of the enclosure is flanked on the inside by a high bank and on the outside by a low bank  
         2. Stage 2 was approximately 100-200 years later  
            1. About 2500 BCE the sarsen stones were brought from the Avebury area of the Marlborough Downs  
            2. They were then arranged inside the circle in a horseshoe-shaped setting of five tall trilithons (paired uprights with a lintel)—the central and largest of which is known as the giant trilithon—surrounded by 30 uprights linked by curved lintels to form a circle.  
         3. Stage 3 was long lasting and began roughly 400-500 years later  
            1. side ditches and banks of a ceremonial avenue almost 2 miles (3 km) long were dug from Stonehenge to the River Avon at some time in the period between 2470 and 2280 BCE.  
      4. Likely built by early farmers of nearby settlements  
      5. Equinox and Solstices  
         1. Equinox \- night  and day are approximately equal  
         2. Solstices \- longest and shortest days of the year, depending on season  
      6. Bluestones  
         1. quarried from the Preseli Hills 160 miles away  
         2. They weigh between 2 and 5 tons each  
      7. Sarsens  
         1. On average the sarsens weigh 25 tons  
         2. Quarried at Marlborough Downs, 20 miles away  
      8. Conservation  
         1. Has undergone restoration and conservation efforts  
            1. 1919, 1958 and 1959, 1964 and 2021  
      9. The site is still a sacred place of special religious and cultural significance for many  
           
9. **The Ambum stone** Ambum Valley, Enga Province, Papua New Guinea. c. 1500 bce. Greywacke. (Papua sounds like Pap-wa)  
   1. Form (visual analysis)  
      1. Oval like form  
      2. Stone coloration  
      3. Rounded bottom, head makes a handle  
      4. Abstracted animal, possibly and well crafted with attention to detail  
         1. Ears, eyes, nose and mouth sculpted in relief (raised)  
      5. Graywacke sandstone is a sedimentary rock that is made up mostly of sand-size grains that were rapidly deposited very near the source rock from which they were weathered.  
   2. Function  
      1.  Appears to be a pestle, other pestles in the same region had human, animal or bird forms for handles  
      2. Had a life as a religious object by the Enga  
         1. Type of cult object where ancestors of the people were believed to reside  
         2. Ward off danger and promote fertility and well being of the tribe  
   3. Content  
      1. Animal of unknown type, possibly an echidna (ah-**kid**\-na) which is a fetus of a breed of anteater  
   4. Context (contextual analysis)  
      1. One of the oldest artworks from the Oceania (O-she-an-ya) region  
      2. Made with stone tools, time consuming to create and must have held great importance for the artist or owner.  
      3. Enga society based on “big man” system, where the ability of an individual to control negotiations, natural resources and supernatural forces determines rank in society  
         1. These leaders negotiate for resources that can be redistributed at religious ceremonies  
      4. Objects were buried in ancestral lands and pig sacrifices were made to appease the spirits contained within the objects  
      5. Prior to the Enga peoples conversion to Christianity, it was being used as a religious object  
      6. Involved in controversy over who owns artifacts  
         1. With the introduction of Christianity and subsequent yielding of control to Australian administration, the objects took on importance of primitive art, therefore had monetary value  
         2. The stone was sold, exported, and eventually offered to both the British and Australian art museums. The New Guinea Art Museum wanted to purchase it, but was unable to afford the $115,000 cost.   
         3. Laws have been in place to prevent the export of objects of antiquity and relevance to Papua New Guinea  
         4. Raises the issue on a larger, global scale

11. **Terra cotta fragment** Lapita. Reef Islands, Solomon Islands. 1000 bce. Terra cotta (incised).  (Laa-pee-ta)  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. Red slip earthenware  
          1. Slip is clay particles and pigment suspended in water and applied to the outside of the pot  
          2. Earthenware is a low fire clay, that is slightly porous  
       2. Probably coil construction possibly using a paddle and anvil technique  
       3. Ornate designs/patterns cover the surface and are filled with a white colorant created from coral lime to contrast the surface color of the pot.   
       4. These pieces contain symmetrical and radial designs created from dots, circles and lines/hatching.   
       5. Most of these pots were simple bowls  
       6. Sand (grog) was added to help reduce shrinkage and breaking during the firing process  
    2. Function  
       1. The pots from which these potsherds come were likely used to serve and store food  
    3. Content  
       1. Geometric designs rich in circles and patterning, along with abstract human forms (anthropomorphic)  
       2. Research has shown that the pottery contained patterns that were part of a visual language that the Lapita artisans adhered to.  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. James Cook and other voyages from the Enlightenment period of the 1700’s speculated about how these islands were populated.   
       2. Since this clay is not indigenous to the Solomon Islands, it indicates an easterly migration of people from Taiwan  
       3. From Taiwan to New Guinea, from there eastward to other Pacific islands  
       4. Stratigraphic archeology of the 1950s demonstrated that Lapitas actually came from areas around Taiwan, not New Guinea
