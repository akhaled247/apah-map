# 93 — The Palace at Versailles

## Attribution

**Culture / Artist:** Louis Le Vau and Jules Hardouin-Mansart (architects). 
**Date:** Begun 1669 CE
**Medium:** Masonry, stone, wood, iron, and gold leaf (architecture); marble and bronze (sculpture); gardens
**Location:** Versailles, France

## Form

- Baroque
- 2300 rooms, 2,153 windows, and takes up 67,000 square meters (12 football fields)
- king’s official state bedroom
- queen’s main bedchamber
- Salon of War and Salon of Peace are decorated with paintings highlighting, unsurprisingly, France’s military might
- Study for Dogs was a room that Louis XV’s valets shared with his dogs, who also got to sleep in a room full of gilding and painted decoration
- Hall of Mirrors, which runs along the entire length of the central building.
  - One wall contains a row of giant windows looking out over the gardens (almost 2,000 acres of manicured lawns, fountains and paths arranged in the formal garden style
  - other wall is covered with 357 mirrors
- basic structure is classical; it’s symmetrical, repetitive, and based on simple elements that are directly borrowed from ancient Greek temples
- Apollo Fountain
- Apollo Salon

## Function

- emphasize Louis’s importance
  - The Sun King
  - everything revolves around me
  - I am the state
- East/West axis so the sun would rise and set in alignment with his home

## Content

- sculpture, painting, and fountains that all focused on himself
- The exterior of the palace utilized classical architecture and was intended to remind people of the greatness of the antique Greek and Roman past/associate that with France and himself
- Sun god/sun king - linking Louis to the Greek god Apollo

## Context

- Team of:
  - Architect
  - Landscape Designer
  - Interior Designer
- Excesses of the like that lead to the French Revolution
- Commissioned by Louis XIV, the “Sun King”
- Completed 21 years after being commissioned in 1661
- Classical structures were used to contain the elaborate grandeur of the Baroque style that was all the rage in the mid-seventeenth century
- Baroque to illustrate the wealth/glory and classical to illustrate intellectual and cultural significance
