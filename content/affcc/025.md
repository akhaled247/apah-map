# 25 — Lamassu from the citadel of Sargon II, Dur Sharrukin

## Attribution

**Culture / Artist:** Neo-Assyrian. 
**Date:** c. 720–705 BCE
**Medium:** Alabaster
**Location:** Khorsabad (modern Iraq)

## Form

- Monolithic stones
- Appearance
- Double horned crown with feathers and rosettes
- Ears of a bull, with earring
- Large unibrow
- Ornate spherical masses of hair, frame each side of the head
- Ornate beard consisting of intricate twisting patterns that are broken up by 6 horizontal bands
- Wings also use repetition of pattern to create feathers
- Patterns run along the underbelly, back, rump and tip of tail
- There are 5 legs in total - 4 when viewed from the side, but 5 when you consider the extra leg when viewed from the front.
- The side view shows the lamassu in motion
- The front view shows it stationary
- The anatomy is well defined and under the surface, there appears to be a skeletal form as well as muscles and circulatory system.
- Size: The winged bull stands at 16 feet square and weighs approximately 40 tons

## Function

- These were guardians of the gateways into the citadel
- As massive structures, they can also be an expression of the power of the king.
- Mave have even served a structural purpose.

## Content

- The lamassu is a composite mythological being with the head of a human, the body and ears of a bull, and the wings of a bird
- Cuneiform writing was carved into the negative spaces surrounding the creature
- The cuneiform, written in the Akkadian language, describes the building of the citadel and giving thanks to the gods

## Context

- The lamassu were carved during the eighth century BCE, during the height of the Assyrian civilization.
- While these sculptures lined each side of the gateways, (even serving a structural purpose). There were additional relief sculptures decorating the interior of the palace.
- Obviously the task of moving and positioning these works was a major undertaking.
- There were relief carvings in the palace that illustrate how the pieces were moved.
