# 14 — Statues of votive figures, from the Square Temple at Eshnunna

## Attribution

**Culture / Artist:** Sumerian. 
**Date:** c. 2700 BCE
**Medium:** Gypsum inlaid with shell and black limestone
**Location:** Tell Asmar (modern Iraq)

## Form

- Sculpted from alabaster, which is a soft rock often used for carving
- figures were buried together, sizes range from 1’ - 3’ in height
- Very stiff form that stands tall and firm
- Arms with the hands clasped together
- Enlarged eyes made from shells with black limestone pupils look straight ahead
- Lips closed
- Detailed hair and garment
- Stylized, non natural proportions

## Function

- To stand gazing at a statue that was believed to be the embodiment  of a god
- These were meant to be worshippers always attentive to the god

## Content

- See Form above

## Context

- figures were buried together in the floor of a temple, believed to be dedicated to the god Abu
- Abu was a god of plants and agriculture
- The temple was in Eshnunna in the northern part of ancient Mesopotamia
- The temples appear to have been non-congregational and access was probably limited to priests. These votives could have represented the priest when he wasn’t there in body
- The upper class of Sumerian culture would commission these votives, and therefore it is believed that some of the characteristics of the patron are included in the carving, though it is not meant to be a portrait
- Very few of the figures have been found in-situ. Most have been found buried or in fragments scattered about
- These figures are considered to be an excellent example of early Sumerian art
- Some of the earliest examples of cities were emerging from the areas around the Tigris and Euphrates rivers.
- With cities came administrative responsibilities and the need for record keeping.
- With those needs came the development of writing
