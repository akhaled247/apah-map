# 22 — Akhenaten, Nefertiti, and three daughters

## Attribution

**Culture / Artist:** New Kingdom (Amarna period), 18th Dynasty, Egypt. 
**Date:** c. 1353–1335 BCE
**Medium:** Limestone
**Location:** Amarna, Egypt

## Form

- Size approximately 15 ⅜” x 17 ⅛”
- Near symmetry
- Sunken (or incised) relief
- Characters have a cartoon-like quality
- Curved, fluid forms. This are not the traditional stiff and static Egyptian forms
- Still have tradition composite view of body/profile of face
- Cuneiform in negative space

## Function

- A religious object, an altarpiece for someone’s home

## Content

- Akhenaten, Nefertiti, and three daughters in very informal setting; this is a very personal look at family life
- Nefertiti’s hat is one worn by goddess and very few queens, thus showing she is the divine queen
- The throne of Nerfertiti also shows symbols of upper and lower Egypt, the lotus and papyrus plants, showing she is queen of both and possesses great power alongside her husband
- Aten is the disc in the top center of the tablet
- Disc has cobra - means this is the one god
- Rays with hands except next to faces of the king and queen, there you find Ankhs, Egyptian symbol for life

## Context

- Akhenaten changed the religion from worshiping Amun to Aten
- Egypt was polytheistic, Akhenaten changes it to monotheistic
- Aten was a minor sun god, represented as a disc with rays
- Akhenaten makes him and his wife the only representatives on earth, and this upsets the priesthood
- Akhenaten changed his name from Amenhotep IV to Akhenaten
- Old name means Amun is pleased; new name means beneficial or servant to Aten
- Moves the capital from Thebes to Tell el-Amarna or Amarna, more than 200 miles (300 km) north
- After thousands of years with little creativity or inventiveness, Egyptian style of art changed
- After his death, Egypt returns to former traditions
