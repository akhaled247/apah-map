# 34 — Doryphoros (Spear Bearer)

## Attribution

**Culture / Artist:** Polykleitos. 
**Date:** Original c. 450–440 BCE; Roman copy 1st century BCE/CE
**Medium:** Roman marble copy of Greek bronze original
**Location:** Pompeii, Italy (original Argos/Athens, Greece)

## Form

- Human, nude male figure; larger than life - 6’11” in height
- Spear bearer - would have originally held a bronze spear
- Polykleitos used a “canon” to develop and replicate the perfect human form
- Example of the classical form as well as contrapposto
- Counter balance of body
- Departure of the archaic stiffness of forms

## Function

- Polykleitos sought to capture the ideal proportions of the human figure in his statues and developed a set of aesthetic principles governing these proportions that was known as the Canon or “Rule.”
- Doryphoros was meant to represent an idealized version of the perfect Greek male citizen

## Content

- Spear bearer (spear is lost)
- The Greek idea of striving for perfection
- An example of contrasts
- Contrapposto
- Weight bearing leg contrasts relaxed
- Relaxed arm vs spear bearing
- Turn of the head, vs turn of the hips

## Context

- This is a Roman Copy of a Greek bronze sculpture
- Roman admiration of Greek work led to copies
- We know about Polykleitos because of Roman copies and primary sources
- Not many bronzes exist as most were melted down for the metal
- The Doryphoros, or Spear-Bearer, has long been regarded as an exemplum of male beauty as conceived of by the ancient Greeks.
- A warrior would not go to battle in the nude, so there is another purpose in mind within the work
- Polykleitos developed a “canon” of proportions to represent the ideal male form, but that text has been lost.
- Sculpture was found in a palestra in Pompeii; this was a place where athletes would train and exercise
