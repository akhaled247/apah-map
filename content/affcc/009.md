# 9 — The Ambum stone

## Attribution

**Culture / Artist:** Enga Province, Papua New Guinea. 
**Date:** c. 1500 BCE
**Medium:** Greywacke
**Location:** Ambum Valley, Papua New Guinea

## Form

- Oval like form
- Stone coloration
- Rounded bottom, head makes a handle
- Abstracted animal, possibly and well crafted with attention to detail
- Ears, eyes, nose and mouth sculpted in relief (raised)
- Graywacke sandstone is a sedimentary rock that is made up mostly of sand-size grains that were rapidly deposited very near the source rock from which they were weathered.

## Function

- Appears to be a pestle, other pestles in the same region had human, animal or bird forms for handles
- Had a life as a religious object by the Enga
- Type of cult object where ancestors of the people were believed to reside
- Ward off danger and promote fertility and well being of the tribe

## Content

- Animal of unknown type, possibly an echidna (ah-kid-na) which is a fetus of a breed of anteater

## Context

- One of the oldest artworks from the Oceania (O-she-an-ya) region
- Made with stone tools, time consuming to create and must have held great importance for the artist or owner.
- Enga society based on “big man” system, where the ability of an individual to control negotiations, natural resources and supernatural forces determines rank in society
- These leaders negotiate for resources that can be redistributed at religious ceremonies
- Objects were buried in ancestral lands and pig sacrifices were made to appease the spirits contained within the objects
- Prior to the Enga peoples conversion to Christianity, it was being used as a religious object
- Involved in controversy over who owns artifacts
- With the introduction of Christianity and subsequent yielding of control to Australian administration, the objects took on importance of primitive art, therefore had monetary value
- The stone was sold, exported, and eventually offered to both the British and Australian art museums. The New Guinea Art Museum wanted to purchase it, but was unable to afford the $115,000 cost.
- Laws have been in place to prevent the export of objects of antiquity and relevance to Papua New Guinea
- Raises the issue on a larger, global scale
