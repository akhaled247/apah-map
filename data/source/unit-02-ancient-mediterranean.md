         1. **Cultural Contexts of Ancient Mediterranean Art**  
            1. Ancient near east consists of:  
               1. Sumerian, Akkadian, Neo-Sumerian and Babylonian, Assyrian, NeoBabylonian, and Persian societies and city-states  
               2. Present day countries of Iraq, Syria, Iran, Turkey, Lebanon, Israel, Palestine, Jordan, and Cyprus  
               3. From 3500 to 330 BCE  
               4. Religion plays a significant role in the art and architecture  
               5. Leaders who proclaim themselves to deities.   
            2. Dynastic Egypt  
               1. Predynastic  
               2. Old Kingdom  
               3. New Kingdom (including the Amarna period)  
                  1. Amarna was important because of the cultural reform and stylistic revolution it ushered in.  
               4. Artwork   
                  1. was created with a sense of permanence  
                  2. Art includes mythological and religious symbolism  
                  3. Cult of the sun is frequent theme  
                  4. Clear distinction between social classes  
                     1. Hierarchical scale  
                     2. Idealized proportions vs. naturalism  
                  5. Artistic canon that was consistently used for many dynasties without much experimentation  
                  6. Innovations were still subject to the accepted canon  
            3. Other cultures  
               1. Greek: Made in current day Greece, Turkey, and southern Italy between 600 BCE \- 100 BCE  
               2. Etruscan: Etruria, Italy 700 BCE \- 100 BCE  
               3. Roman: Europe and Western Asia, 753 BCE \- 337 CE  
            4. Periods of Greek art are Archaic, Classical, and Hellenistic, each defined by stylistic traditions.  
            5. Etruscan: even though Etruria was composed of various city states, it is classified as a single cultural unit.  
            6. Roman art includes works from the republican, early imperial, late imperial, and late antique periods, as defined using governmental structures and dynasties rather than stylistic characteristics. (I think in addition to, not rather than)  
            7. The Greek, Etruscan, and Roman cultures shared a rich tradition of epic storytelling involving gods, goddesses, and heros.   
            8. Greek architecture and figural representations are characterized by idealized proportions. These were in keeping with societal ideas of harmony and order.  
         2. Materials, Processes, Techniques   
            1. Art showed humans interacting with gods using certain stylistic conventions  
               1. Combined frontal and ¾ views  
               2. Use of hierarchical scale  
               3. Dividing the composition into registers  
            2. The Egyptian architectural construction of the clerestory is particularly important for the history of architecture.  
            3.  Pyramids and rock cut tombs and temples were important and showed the importance of pharaohs. (“god-king” supposedly descended from the sun god.  
            4. Etruscan and Roman periods used eclectic iconography and portraiture.  
            5. Roman architecture borrowed heavily from Etruscans and Greeks. It also was influenced by changes in technology.  
         3. **Interactions Within and Across Cultures in Ancient Mediterranean Art**  
            1. The exchange of ideas are dependent on a variety of factors \- trade, war, travel, conquest, etc.  
            2. The works of this period show active exchange of ideas and subsequent influence on the classical world.  
            3. A study of works from Egypt and Near East provide a basis for comparative study both in this region and beyond.  
            4. Ancient Greek, Etruscan, and Roman artists and architects were influenced by earlier Mediterranean cultures.  
            5. Greek architecture and objects influenced both Etruscan and Roman work.  
            6. Many Hellenistic works are Roman in origin.  
         4. **Purpose and Audience in Ancient Mediterranean Art**  
            1. Artistic traditions of the ancient Near East and dynastic Egypt  
               1. Focus on funerary and palatial items  
               2. Divinities and royalties  
            2. Architectural structures  
               1. Ziggurats for worship   
               2. Massive palaces demonstrate power/authority of ruler  
            3. Funerary objects of Egypt  
               1. Ka statues that were meant to house the spirit of the dead  
               2. Artifacts, decorations, furnishings for tombs  
            4. The art of Ancient Greece and Rome is grounded in civic ideals and polytheism  
            5. Etruscan and ancient Roman art express

                  1. Society  
                  2. Religion  
                  3. Power  
                  4. State before self  
               2. preference for conspicuous display (obvious display)

         5. **Theories and Interpretations of Ancient Mediterranean Art**  
            1. Can change over time with new in research/information  
            2. Can be created through visual analysis and info from other disciplines, technologies, available evidence

12. **White Temple and its ziggurat** Uruk (modern Warka, Iraq). Sumerian. c. 3500–3000 bce. Mud brick. 2 images  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. Ziggurat \- it is a mound shaped similar to a pyramid with the top cut off.   
          1. Made from mud bricks serving as a base for the temple  
          2. Total height of Z & T is approximately 40’  
          3. Flat top was bitumen covered with brick to serve as foundation for the temple  
       2. Temple \- a structure built for the purpose of religious activities  
          1. Tripartite design  
             1. Rectangular central hall flanked by rooms on either side  
             2. Three entrances \- none faced ramp, required  
          2. Corners were aligned to the cardinal directions  
          3. Whitewashed inside and out  
    2. Function  
       1. Religious \- Temples was dedicated to deities  
       2. The ziggurat \+ tower could be seen high above the city as a symbol of power to those approaching  
       3. Heart of the theocratic political system  
          1. Theocracy is a government where a god is ruler, and state officials work on his behalf.  
          2. A ziggurat is a visual connection to the god/goddess honored there, and the deity’s political authority.  
       4. Z & T’s Symbolized bridges between earth and heavens.  
       5. Meeting place for humans and their gods.  
    3. Content  
       1. This Z & T was dedicated to the sky god Anu  
          1. Anu was believed to be the supreme source of all authority, for the other gods and for all mortal rulers  
          2. Ritually buried bones of a leopard and a lion  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. Ziggurats were common to Sumerians, Babylonians and Assyrians  
       2. Sumerians built before Egyptians built pyramids, though they were less impressive than pyramids  
       3. Quite likely that slavery was used for the construction  
       4. Uruk  
          1. City life began more than 5,000 years ago  
          2. Area where writing emerged  
          3. Great importance to southern Mesopotamia  
          4. Uruk was initially ruled by a priest-king, and it is believed that many early Mesopotamian cities followed the same model

13. **Palette of King Narmer** Predynastic Egypt. c. 3000–2920 bce. Greywacke. 2 images  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. 25” in height  
       2. Contains 3 registers on the back and 4 on the front  
       3. Central placement of Narmer  
       4. Hierarchical scale  
       5. Composite views typical of Egyptian art (all the body's features but the torso are shown in profile)  
       6. Line of symmetry runs vertically on both sides  
    2. Function  
       1. Ceremonial palette used in the ritual mixing and application of the King’s eye makeup  
       2. Gives Narmer the status of being equal to a god  
    3. Content  
       1. Depicts the unification of upper and lower Egypt  
       2. Back registers  
          1. Narmer wearing crown of Upper Egypt, while killing or possibly sacrificing and enemy with a mace (club)  
          2. Two enemy dead in the lower register  
          3. Upper register has two images of Hathor looking down. Narmer’s name appears between the two images of Hathor  
          4. Falcon representing god Horus  
          5. Man’s head emerges from papyrus flowers (papyrus flowers represent Lower Egypt  
          6. The servant on left holds Narmer’s sandals  
       3. Front registers  
          1. Top register is repeated  
          2. Second register  
             1. Victory in battle with Narmer wearing the red crown of Lower Egypt  
             2. Bodies of enemies with heads decapitated  
          3. Third register shows Narmer’s servants controlling 2 lionesses with leashes.   
             1. Narmer controls servants who in turn control the cats.   
             2. This declares the king’s authority  
          4. Bottom register shows Narmer as a bull. (Egyptian gods could change shapes). He is crushing a rebellious town.  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. This object was found ritually buried with other items that had been used for ceremonial purposes  
       2. Items ritually buried were offerings to gods. It was meant to demonstrate piety on the part of the donor and improve standing with the god.  
       3. Upper and Lower Egypt  
          1. Lower Egypt is to the north  
          2. Upper is to the south.   
          3. Names reflect the flow of the Nile River, from South to North.   
          4. Prior to unification, most Egyptians lived in one of two different kingdoms. They shared some cultural norms, but were under different rulers.  
          5. Symbols of Egypt:  
             1. Uraeus/Cobra symbolized Lower Egypt while a Vulture represented Upper Egypt. Both were symbols of protection.  
             2. Plants: Lotus symbolized Upper Egypt; Papyrus, Lower Egypt.

14. **Statues of votive figures**, from the Square Temple at Eshnunna (modern Tell Asmar,Iraq) Sumerian. c. 2700 black limestone.  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. Sculpted from alabaster, which is a soft rock often used for carving  
       2. 12 figures were buried together, sizes range from 1’ \- 3’ in height  
       3. Very stiff form that stands tall and firm  
       4. Arms with the hands clasped together  
       5. Enlarged eyes made from shells with black limestone pupils look straight ahead  
       6. Lips closed  
       7. Detailed hair and garment  
       8. Stylized, non natural proportions  
    2. Function  
       1. To stand gazing at a statue that was believed to be the embodiment  of a god  
       2. These were meant to be worshippers always attentive to the god  
    3. Content  
       1. See Form above  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. 12 figures were buried together in the floor of a temple, believed to be dedicated to the god Abu  
          1. Abu was a god of plants and agriculture  
       2. The temple was in Eshnunna in the northern part of ancient Mesopotamia  
       3. The temples appear to have been non-congregational and access was probably limited to priests. These votives could have represented the priest when he wasn’t there in body  
       4. The upper class of Sumerian culture would commission these votives, and therefore it is believed that some of the characteristics of the patron are included in the carving, though it is not meant to be a portrait  
       5. Very few of the figures have been found in-situ. Most have been found buried or in fragments scattered about  
       6. These figures are considered to be an excellent example of early Sumerian art   
       7. Some of the earliest examples of cities were emerging from the areas around the Tigris and Euphrates rivers.  
          1. With cities came administrative responsibilities and the need for record keeping.   
          2. With those needs came the development of writing

15. **Seated scribe Saqqara** Egypt. Old Kingdom, Fourth Dynasty. c. 2620–2500 bce. Painted limestone  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. This is a painted limestone sculpture with eyes made from minerals and wooden nipples  
       2. Seated, frontal facing, legs crossed attentive, weaning a kilt and ready to write  
       3. Hands in writing position and holding a papyrus. The right hand is missing the reed brush  
       4. Slightly pudgy body  
    2. Function  
       1. This sculpture has a frontal appearance and is most likely meant for placement in a tomb  
       2. Possible this was to serve as dwelling for scribes “ka” in afterlife  
          1. A ka statue is a type of ancient Egyptian statue intended to provide a resting place for the ka (life-force or spirit) of the person after death.  
       3. This sculpture may provide a scribe for pharaoh in afterlife, since it was possibly placed in pharaoh’s tomb  
    3. Content  
       1. His body suggests that he doesn’t perform manual labor  
       2. Crossed legs would have been typical posture for a scribe  
       3. This informal pose and natural representation would have never been used for a pharaoh  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. Originally discovered in 1850 by the French archaeologist Auguste Mariette in a necropolis in Saqqara (sa-car-uh)  
       2. The exact location of the find is unknown, as Mariette’s records have been lost  
       3. Scribes were highly regarded as most of the population was unable to read or write

16. **Standard of Ur** from the Royal Tombs at Ur (modern Tell el-Muqayyar, Iraq) Sumerian. c. 2600–2400 bce. Wood inlaid with shell, lapis lazuli, and red limestone. 2 images  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. When viewed from the front or back, it is rectangular, but when seen from the end, it appears as a truncated triangle (shortened or missing the tip in this case)  
       2. Size is approximately 8 ⅝” tall and approximately 19 ⅞” in length  
       3. Lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, Shells from Persian Gulf, Red limestone from India  
       4. Three registers on each side  
       5. Hierarchical scale used for ruler  
       6. Figures represented have broad shoulders with heads and feet in profile  
       7. Large eyes similar to the votive figures from Eshnunna  
       8. There had been decay from burial, so it has been restored   
    2. Function  
       1. Real function is unknown, but one theory suggests that it was carried on a pole and hence the name.   
       2. Another suggests it was the soundbox of a musical instrument  
    3. Content  
       1. War Side  
          1. Bottom register \- chariots are trampling the enemy  
          2. Middle register \- soldiers on left side, middle they are killing enemy and right naked enemy are being marched to king  
          3. Top register \- King is center with enemy being presented on right  
       2. Peace side  
          1. Lower two registers: Preparations being made for celebration  
          2. Upper register: King is in the upper left register. Note hierarchical scale. Guests and musicians on right  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. Materials used in the construction of the Standard of Ur were luxurious and hard to obtain, showing the patron’s wealth/status  
       2. Object was found in one of the largest graves from the Royal cemetery at Ur. Positioned at the right shoulder of the man buried there  
       3. Ur is famous as the home of the Old Testament patriarch Abraham (Genesis 11:29–32), there is no actual proof that Tell el-Muqayyar was identical with “Ur of the Chaldees.”  
       4. “Nanna” was the moon goddess and the patron deity for the city-state of Ur

17. **Great Pyramids (Menkaura, Khafre, Khufu) and Great Sphinx** Giza, Egypt. Old Kingdom, Fourth Dynasty. c. 2550–2490 bce. Cut limestone. 2 images  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. Great Pyramids and Sphinx are part of a necropolis complex  
       2. The complex also contains smaller pyramids for the queens and mastabas for other elites as well as lesser cemeteries  
       3. There is a valley temple to the east of each pyramid, connected by a long causeway to a funerary temple at the base of the pyramid  
       4. Pyramids are in alignment   
    2. Function  
       1. Funerary, burial of the pharaohs  
    3. Content  
       1. Items that the pharaoh would need in the afterlife were placed in the pyramid  
       2. The shape was a possible reference to the sun, sides representing the rays pointing upward, but also a ramp upward for the pharaoh.  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. The location of the necropolis complex where the pyramids reside is near the west banks of the Nile river, at the edge of modern day Cairo  
       2. The largest and oldest of the 3 was built for Khufu, next was Kahfre and then the smallest, for Menkaura  
       3. After their construction, the pyramids remained the tallest structures for over 3 thousand years.  
       4. Construction is thought to have skilled labor and possibly slave labor  
          1. Speculation exists over how the stones were transported an lifted into position  
          2. There has been a discovery of a town for workers south of the complex  
       5. Egypt is not the only place with pyramids. Other African countries as well as Mesopotamia have pyramids or step pyramids. There was an obvious exchange of ideas from travel and trade.  
       6. With their ascension to the throne, pharaohs took on a near divine nature. The “ka” or spirit of kingship was often depicted next to the pharaoh. The god Horus (the falcon) was associated with the living king.  
       7. In modern times, urban encroachment has made its way up to the complex almost up to the sphinx. With it, problems of pollution, waste, illegal activity, auto traffic threaten the preservation of the site.

18. **King Menkaura and Queen** Old Kingdom, Fourth Dynasty. c. 2490–2472 bce. Greywacke.  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. Dimensions: 56 x 22 1/2 x 21 3/4 in.  
       2. Stiff/rigid forms, arms at side, left foot forward  
       3. Stylized/Idealized bodies  
       4. King is wearing nemes and a false beard and wrap around kilt, all symbols of his position.  
       5. Queen wearing a tight, sheer dress  
       6. Traces of paint remain  
       7. The queen’s foot is also slightly forward, uncommon for Egyptian sculptures  
       8. Unusual for a pharaoh’s image, the king has no protective cobra (known as a uraeus) perched on his brow. This absence suggests that both the king’s nemes and the queen’s wig were originally covered in a sheath of precious metal and that the cobra would have been part of that addition.  
       9. The slab behind that joins the two forms, giving strength to the sculpture  
    2. Function  
       1. This is a funerary item as it was found in Kenkaura’s valley temple  
       2. Some suggest that it was meant to house the “ka”  
    3. Content  
       1. The base is left unfinished and uninscribed. This is usually where the names of the individuals are placed. It is assumed that it is King Menkaura as it was found in Menkaura's Valley Temple and because it resembles other statues from the same findspot bearing his name.  
       2. The name of the women is unknown, Menkaura had 3 wives. Some have suggested that it was the goddess Hathor, but she exhibits no divine qualities. Some think it may have been his mother, as some later kings were shown with their mothers. But, in private sculptures where a man and woman are shown, and names indicated, it is usually husband and wife.   
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. Pharaohs wore false beards that were often passed down. It was an association with the god Osiris and a symbol of their rise to divine status. Both men and women pharaohs wore them  
       2. Valley Temples were used to perpetuate the cult of the deceased king and were active places of worship for hundreds of years (sometimes much longer) after the king’s death.

19. **The Code of Hammurabi** Babylon (modern Iran). Susian. c. 1792–1750 bce. Basalt.  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. Stele, almost 7’ in height, with carved relief at top  
       2. The writing is engraved cuneiform in the Akkadian language  
       3. Composite views \- front view of torso, profile of rest of body  
       4. Sun god at top shows his divine nature by the horned crown and rays that emanate from his shoulders  
    2. Function  
       1. Elevation of Hammurabi to near deity status  
       2. Records laws for equal treatment of people  
    3. Content  
       1. Pictured at the top is Hammurabi with Shamash, the Babylonian sun god and god of justice.  
          1. Hammurabi is receiving ring and scepter from the sun god demonstrating the divine nature of the law and the god’s approval of Hammurabi  
       2. Record of laws  
          1. Probably not written by Hammurabi, but legal precedents  
          2. Laws pertaining to everyday life, written in common language  
    4. Context (contextual analysis)  
       1. Captured in the 12th century BCE and moved from Sippar to Susa, where it was discovered in 1901 and moved to the Louvre Museum  
       2. Historians believe the Elamite King Shutruk-Nahhunte plundered the four-ton slab during a 12th century B.C. raid on the Babylonian city of Sippar 

       

20. **Temple of Amun-Re and Hypostyle Hall** Karnak, near Luxor, Egypt. New Kingdom, 18th and 19th Dynasties. Temple: c. 1550 bce; hall: c. 1250 bce. Cut sandstone and mud brick. 3 images  
    1. Form (visual analysis)  
       1. The main temple of Amun-Re had two axes—one that went north/south and the other that extended east/west.  
       2. The temple is an example of a hypostyle hall \- this is a space with a roof supported by columns  
          1. There are 134 columns supporting the roof  
          2. The 12 center columns are 69’ tall (roughly 6 ⅓  stories high)  
          3. The column would have been brightly painted  
       3. The center of the hall is taller than the two sides allowing for clerestory illumination of the hall  
    2. Function  
       1. Karnak was a place of worship, but also a place for the god Amun-Re to dwell on earth.  
       2. Residence for priests  
    3. Content  
       1. Conceptually, Egyptian temples were related to the creation of the world  
          1. They were visual representations of the “mound of creation” emerging from primeval waters  
          2. Ceilings represented the sky and were often painted with birds  
          3. Columns were designed with lotus, papyrus, and palm plants in order to reflect the marsh-like environment of creation

    4. Context (contextual analysis)

46. **Pantheon** Imperial Roman. 118–125 ce. Concrete with stone facing. 2 images  
    1. Form  
       1. The original version was thought to be a rectangular temple, Greek in its plan.  
       2. The latest version has the front of a traditional temple with pediment, 16 columns and the pediment names Marcus Agrippa as the patron.   
       3. The front of the building is a portico, with monolithic Corinthian columns, and a pediment  
       4. The large bronze doors to the cella, measuring 14.6 ft wide by 24.7 ft high  
       5. It is a rotunda; in other words, a round building  
       6. There were niches in the walls that would have held sculptures of gods and deified emperors.  
          1. They were framed with a column on the left and right  
          2. Those columns supported an entablature above which there is a curved pediment.  
       7. It has a domed interior with a coffered ceiling  
          1. The Pantheon’s dome is the largest in the ancient world, 142 feet across.  
          2. Built with concrete using various size and weights of rock to control strength and weight of the dome  
          3. The oculus (27’ in diameter) helps with weight concerns but also provides  light for the interior.  
          4. Walls are some 20’ thick to help bear the weight and outward thrust of the dome.  
          5. The dome is a hemisphere; the building was designed such that a perfect sphere would fit from the floor to the top of the dome.    
       8. The frieze above the columns has a series of false windows with pediments alternating with rectangular shapes. These do not align with the coffered ceiling, which creates a dynamic feel, almost as if the dome is rotating.  
       9. Rectangles, squares and geometric shapes are used throughout the interior  
       10. Colored marble  
    2. Function  
       1. Temple  
       2. Church  
       3. Various individuals have been buried there.  
       4. To instill a sense of pride in Roman culture  
    3. Content  
       1. Name: a temple for all Roman deities  
          1. prefix “pan” meaning "all", "of everything", or "involving all members" of a group.   
          2. Theon means *godly* and is of Greek origin  
       2. Design is about earth and heavenly spheres meeting  
          1. Gods surrounding   
          2. Dome representing heavens  
          3. Oculus the sun  
       3. However \- the above information is now under question. New findings indicate that it may not have been a temple, but had other uses.   
       4. Expense of materials (colored marble) shows wealth and power of the Roman Empire to reach across the world and import materials  
       5. Movement of light from the oculus, through the space of the rotunda  
    4. Context  
       1. Originally built by Marcus Agrippa in 27 BC, son-in-law of Emperor Augustus) in 27 BCE.; burning in 80 CE.  
       2. Rebuilt or restored in the same location by Emperor Domitian (emperor from 81-96 CE) ; but struck by lightning and burned again in 110 CE  
       3. It was thought that Emperor Hadrian was ruler when construction began on the final version, but new research has shown that it might have been his predecessor, Trajan.  
       4. The Pantheon has been in continuous use since its final construction in c AD 126-128  
    5. The Pantheon fell into disrepair, as did many Roman buildings, after Constantine moved the center of the empire from Rome to Constantinople.  
       1. With the rise of Chirstianity, the building was abandoned and pillaged by the Goths  
       2. In 609 CE, it was dedicated as a Christian church, possibly saving it from ruin as the church spent a great deal repairing and maintaining the structure.   
       3. It was the first Roman temple to be consecrated as a church.  
    6. Pantheon continues to function as a church, as well as a major tourist destination   
    7. This is one of the most copied buildings in the Renaissance and in modern times.  
       1. Thomas Jefferson modeled both Monticello—his home near Charlottesville, Virginia—as well as the Rotunda building at the University of Virginia, after the Pantheon.
